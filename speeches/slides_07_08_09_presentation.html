Thank you, Srijon. I will now examine the prior literature and why earlier claims looked so convincing on paper.

"If you followed AI research throughout 2023, it felt like every quarter brought a shiny new framework claiming language models could fix their own mistakes. It was the same excitement we see today whenever rumors drop about unreleased models like Fable 5.1 or secret codenames. Everyone wanted to believe that self-correction was a solved problem."

Look at the four timeline cards across 2023 on our slide.

In Q1, Kim and colleagues published RCI, claiming a 7% boost on math and reasoning by prompting the model to recursively critique its own code.

In Q2, Shinn and colleagues introduced Reflexion, claiming an 11% improvement by storing verbal reflections across multiple trials.

In Q3, Madaan and colleagues introduced Self-Refine, reporting a 10% gain using structured feedback checklists.

And in Q4, Du and colleagues claimed Multi-Agent Debate gave another 4% boost by having multiple instances debate divergent answers.

On the surface, these numbers looked great. But look at the callout at the bottom of the slide: what the timeline hides.

When you inspect the experimental setups, none of these gains came from intrinsic self-correction. Both RCI and Reflexion relied on oracle leakage, meaning the test harness secretly told the model when it got an answer wrong. Self-Refine relied on a prompt trick, where rules were omitted in the initial prompt and only provided during feedback. And Multi-Agent Debate simply burned three to six times more compute.

Closed Loop vs Grounded Loop

Same model, different information: why internal reflection fails but external verification works (Slide 08/24)

3-Second Presenter Glance Guide LOOK AT SCREEN
Left Box: INTRINSIC (Closed Loop)
1. Generate → LLM (Frozen) → 2. Self-Critique → 3. Revise Blind
Accuracy Change: -1.3% to -27.5%
Zero new facts. Shared weights and prompt bias invent errors.
Right Box: EXTERNAL (Grounded Loop)
LLM Drafts Code → Executes in Python Tool → Traceback Feedback
Accuracy Change: +7.0% to +15.0%
Deterministic reality. Concrete execution errors guide genuine fixes.
Spoken Script [Slide Cue: Switch to Slide 08]

This slide illustrates the core distinction that prior literature often conflated: a closed loop versus a grounded loop.

Look at the red box on the left. This is the closed loop, which is the exact focus of our paper. The model generates an initial answer, receives an unguided prompt to find flaws, and attempts a blind revision.

Notice the fundamental flaw: zero new information enters the system. The weights of the model are frozen. You are asking the exact same network that made the error to spot its own error.

"Anyone who has experimented with language models knows what happens next. If you ask an LLM, 'Are you sure about that?', it immediately panics, apologizes for a mistake it never made, and turns a correct answer into a completely broken one."

Because of this compliance bias and lack of external truth, intrinsic self-correction consistently decreases accuracy by 1.3% to 27.5%.

Now look at the green box on the right. This is the grounded loop, and this is where self-correction genuinely works.

Here, the model writes executable code or equations and runs them in a deterministic environment like a Python interpreter. When the execution fails with an exact traceback, the model conditions its update on objective ground truth. That produces real gains between 7% and 15%.

Core Principle: "A frozen model cannot act as an independent verifier of its own output without external grounding."

Spoken Script [Slide Cue: Switch to Slide 09]

On this slide, we present the forensic breakdown of prior claims.

Look at the paired bar chart on the left. The red bars represent the claimed accuracy improvements from earlier literature. The green bars represent what happens when you run a controlled, scientifically rigorous test.

Notice the striking pattern: every single red bar collapses straight down into green, ending up at or below zero.

Let us walk through the three cards on the right.

First, Oracle Leakage. Papers like RCI and Reflexion reported gains of around 10.5%. But their evaluation harness secretly used ground truth labels to shield correct answers. The model was only prompted to self-correct when its answer was already wrong.

"That is like taking a multiple choice exam where the teacher only taps your desk when you pick the wrong answer and lets you guess again. In real applications, you do not have an oracle. When you prompt the model on all questions, accuracy drops by 2.5%."

Second, the Compute Gap. Multi-Agent Debate claimed a 4% gain. But running three agents through rounds of debate consumes 6x the token budget. When the authors matched compute by giving single-agent Self-Consistency the exact same number of sample calls, standard majority voting beat debate by 1.5%.

Third, the Prompt Trick. Self-Refine reported a 10% gain. However, the initial prompt omitted critical formatting rules, which were only introduced in the feedback prompt. When you provide the complete prompt from the start, the self-correction gain drops to exactly 0.0%.

Read the chart: red collapses to green at or below zero. No confounder survives a fair test.
